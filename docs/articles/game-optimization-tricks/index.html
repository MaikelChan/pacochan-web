Game optimization tricks and anecdotes

To start this year 2024, I thought of writing about various techniques, tricks and curiosities on how to optimize a game, based on what I have seen in the projects I port to consoles. Some tricks are not so well known and others may sound a bit obvious, but you would be surprised how many times I have come across developers making such mistakes.

Many people don’t notice many of these problems because they develop only on PC, and a PCs usually has enough raw power to process whatever you throw at them without any problems. But if you then proceed to port to consoles… get ready for some fun.

Although most of the games I port are made in Unity and the examples I’m going to show are also made with this engine, what I’m going to say can be applied to any engine.

Textures
#

It is obvious that if the textures have less resolution, they will take less memory and therefore less loading times or out of memory crashes, at the cost of looking more blurred or pixelated. But there are certain interesting details that people often miss.

Compression
#

It is important to watch that the dimensions of the textures have sizes multiples of 4: The compression formats that are used to have textures compressed efficiently in the memory of the GPU (VRAM), as for example DXT, ETC, PVRTC or ASTC, keep their data in blocks of 4x4. If the dimensions of a texture do not have a size multiple of 4, that texture will remain totally uncompressed in the VRAM, taking much more space.

Practically all the games I have ported have had this problem to a greater or lesser extent, but a curious example was a visual novel that had some backgrounds at 1920x1080… but not all of them. There were quite a few that by mistake were 1920x1079. This “small” difference made that these textures went from taking only 1MB of memory thanks to the compression, to taking 5.9MB in spite of their dimensions being a little smaller. As the game loaded a lot of backgrounds at the same time, it caused the Nintendo Switch to crash due to lack of memory. Luckily fixing it was very easy, rescaling the backgrounds to 1920x1080, and therefore being able to be compressed.

Scale
#

Even if we had plenty of memory, we must be careful about using large textures if they are always going to be seen at a smaller scale on the screen. One game used huge images for avatars that appeared small on the HUD during gameplay. When a large texture is displayed at reduced size it can cause aliasing. In 3D objects this is usually improved with mipmaps, but in HUDs, menus and interfaces in general, knowing at what size the elements are always going to be seen, and given that they are 2D and there is no perspective, we will avoid the aliasing problems if we reduce the size of the texture approximately to the real size at which it is going to be seen in screen.

Channel packing
#

The material of an object is usually composed of several textures that represent the color, relief, roughness, metalness, ambient occlusion, light emission, etc. The textures of some of these elements, such as roughness, ambient occlusion and metalness, are in black and white. Channel packing would be to combine these three textures in the R, G and B components of a color texture, thus optimizing the rendering. Then with a shader those three components can be separated again to obtain the data of the three original textures.

Memory
#

This may sound a bit obvious… but don’t load the entire game into memory. I’ve come across two games that load absolutely all the data into memory, and by all the data I mean ALL of it. All levels, all textures, all 3D models, all audio, etc. And neither the Switch, PS4 or Xbox One could even boot them.

What they intended was to optimize loading times and prevent the game from stuttering during gameplay due to having to load assets. In fact, loading things ahead of time is a very correct solution… but without going overboard!

One of the games in question was divided into 5 levels, and they were very heavy. Lots of assets, lots of effects and lots of plugins from the Unity asset store. Both my boss and the developer himself didn’t expect the game to be ported to Switch. But I didn’t feel like giving up. It took a lot of work, and with the developer’s help, I had to redo a lot of the game’s systems, all its asset management, and even fix memory leaks. The game was made in a way that made it very difficult to separate the data for each level. But I was finally able to make each level unload all its data after completion. That and with a LOT of optimizations of graphics, audio and…. everything basically, finally the game was playable from start to finish. On top of that, the last level was the worst, because it was bigger and with more stuff than the other four combined… I think I spent almost a month working on that level alone. But I got it, and at 30fps stable on Switch, PS4, and Xbox One, and at 60fps on PS5 and Xbox Series.

So please, be careful about loading everything at once in medium to large games.

Sound
#

A very simple way to improve the memory consumption of music is to set it to Streaming mode. Music and certain ambient sounds are usually the longest types of audio, and therefore, the ones that consume more memory. In Streaming mode, the entire audio is not loaded, but small chunks of it are loaded as it is played. But this consumes a little more CPU, so it is not recommended to use this mode in short audios or those that play repeatedly and frequently.

Draw calls
#

The graphics rendering process starts from the CPU. The engine code will gather every frame all the objects that are visible on the screen and will start sending commands to the GPU for it to know what to render (draw a certain 3D object, with a certain shader, with certain lighting parameters, with certain textures, etc). Each drawing command is a draw call, and sending commands to the GPU has a cost, so the less the better.

Generally speaking, the idea is that there shouldn’t be too many small objects or objects with too many different materials. Each object and each material on that object would generate a draw call. It is better to try to combine the textures of each element of the object into one, its materials and also its 3D models.

To give an example, a street lamp I saw in a game had each of its parts, including the screws that anchor it to the ground in the form of individual objects. The CPU generated about ten draw calls per street lamp, which was totally unnecessary, and could be reduced to just one. It was only required to combine all the pieces in a 3D modeling program.

There are other factors that can increase the number of draw calls, such as the number of lights and shadows that affect an object. So keep an eye on that too.

Overdraw
#

This is a concept that many people don’t know about and it is very important. If a game has performance problems due to graphics, many people resort to reducing the amount of polygons or texture resolution, even if that is not the problem. It is much easier to have performance problems due to overdraw than to excess polygons.

Overdraw is, as it sounds, the drawing of pixels multiple times. For every pixel on the screen, the GPU does a certain amount of work to do the calculations necessary to determine the color of that pixel. The shader would be the code running on the GPU that determines the color, based on the shape of the object, lighting, texture, reflections, shadows, etc. If an object is drawn on the screen, for example a cube, and then another one is drawn in front of it to cover it, a sphere for example, all the pixels that cover those of the cube will have to be drawn twice: once for the cube and once for the sphere. That means having wasted time drawing part of the cube if it is going to be hidden anyway.

With opaque objects, most engines optimize this automatically by drawing the objects in order from closest to the camera to farthest away. Going back to the example above, if the sphere is drawn first and then the cube behind it, the GPU will check via the Z-buffer that there are a number of pixels on the cube that the sphere will cover, so the GPU won’t bother to calculate them.

However, there may be exceptions to this, since sorting objects from closest to farthest is not 100% reliable. Here is an example:

Here are two intersecting objects. The large one on the right covers a good part of the one on the left. But the point of origin of the object on the right (its center in this case), is actually farther back than the point of origin of the object on the left. If the object on the right covers the one on the left, it is only because it is larger. So, for the engine, the one on the right is farther away than the one on the left, so it renders it later. With the help of a graphics debugger like RenderDoc, we can see the overdraw. The intersecting part of the two objects looks lighter pink. That means that all those pixels are being rendered twice unnecessarily. In a case like this it is difficult or impossible to fix the overdraw 100%, but sometimes there are tricks to mitigate it, as we will see later.

But with transparent or semi-transparent objects you have to be careful. In those you can see the objects behind them, so the GPU will not be able to do any optimization with the Z-buffer, but will always re-render all pixels of each semi-transparent object. Tree leaves, bushes, particle systems, etc. can very easily destroy the frame rate of the game.

First example: 3D scene with vegetation
#

Let’s take as an example this night scene of a park:

This game was originally running at 2fps on Switch. I had to optimize a lot of things to get it to 30fps stable, but one of them, and a very important one, was to reduce the overdraw. With a graphics debugger I was able to confirm that the Switch GPU was bottlenecked. Let’s take a look at the overdraw view provided by the debugger in that scene:

This image represents that there are areas with a lot of overdraw. The ground has hardly any, it looks purple, but as we look towards the leaves of the trees, the colors become more intense, going through blues, yellows, reds and whites.

Two main things I did are:

  • All the polygons representing the leaves of the trees and the bushes, were set up to render two-sided. In a scene with so much vegetation it meant a lot of overlapping polygons. So I disabled two-sided rendering for all of them. That made the trees look weird and much less leafy, as they were not meant to have their leaves visible from one side. So I manually edited the 3D model of each tree, strategically repositioning the polygons, rotating them and even deleting some that were redundant, so that the trees looked leafy again but with the extra benefit of reducing the overdraw by about half.

  • The terrain, despite being opaque, generated overdraw in some places. Because it is large, Unity could not correctly determine which areas of the terrain were in front or behind some objects, depending on the camera angle. Since the terrain generally marks the lowest part of the stage, the floor of it, I forced Unity to render it after all the other objects. This can be easily done by increasing the Render Queue of the terrain material. Other engines will have another way to do the same thing. Thanks to that and the magic of the Z-buffer, by rendering the terrain after everything else, the GPU can determine which areas of the screen contain objects that would cover the terrain, so it can ignore rendering it in all those areas.

However, thanks to the previous optimizations, I was able to avoid having to reduce the drawing distance much more than necessary. And this is how the overdraw view finally looks like:

Second example: 2D scene
#

In 2D games it is also quite important, because they usually have many semi-transparent textures. Let’s take a simple scene with a sky and three layers of mountains, each one made with a rectangle with a texture applied (first image). The transparent parts of the mountain texture are an unnecessary cost. Although they are invisible, the GPU processes the whole rectangle (second image). That is why it is usually preferable to add more polygons to the rectangle and give it the approximate shape of the texture (third image), because removing those invisible areas more than compensates in terms of performance than the tiny cost of processing a few more polygons.

In fact, some engines like Unity already do this automatically. Applying a texture to a Sprite Renderer will generate a polygon in the shape of the texture.

Third example: particle system
#

If there are dips in the frame rate when looking at certain particle effects, you have to watch out if there’s an excess with the number of particles or with the complexity of the particle shader. This example is of a simple smoke effect:

The smoke on the left generates a lot of overdraw since it is generating 100 particles per second. It can be easily optimized by reducing the number of particles, and increasing the opacity and particle size to compensate for the reduction in particle density. The result is the smoke on the right, with a considerable reduction in particles (8 per second) but still looking quite close to the original.

Conclusion
#

All of these are just a few things that I remembered, and I may update the article if I come up with more.

Whatever it is, it is always advisable to use the right tools to identify performance problems, such as profilers and debuggers. Avoid simplifying graphics or reducing textures without knowing if they are really the problem.
